Lease-to-own and payment plans

5 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

A small company that cannot find $8,000 this month can often find $400 a month for two years. Offering that option does not lower your price. It widens the group of people who can pay it.

Three structures

DomainEasy, a financing service, publishes the three shapes most plans take, along with the price ranges each tends to suit:

  • Lease-to-own. Equal monthly payments for a fixed term. The simplest form, used mostly for names from about $1,000 to $50,000.
  • Deposit plan. A larger first payment, then equal instalments on the balance. Common from about $50,000 to $500,000, where the seller wants real commitment up front.
  • Balloon plan. Lower monthly payments with a large final payment. Used at the top of the market, where the buyer expects funding or revenue to arrive before the end of the term.

In all three the buyer normally gets use of the name from the first payment, and ownership passes only after the last.

Should a plan cost more than cash?

Usually yes, modestly. You are carrying risk and waiting for your money. A premium of 10% to 25% over the cash price across a one-to-three-year term is easy to explain and easy to accept. Show both numbers, so the saving for paying in full is visible. Some sellers charge no premium at all and treat the plan purely as a way to close more sales. That is a legitimate choice on lower-priced names.

What protects the seller

  • The name stays out of the buyer’s control until it is paid for. Either it remains in an account you or the platform controls with the nameservers pointed where the buyer asks, or it sits with an escrow holding service.
  • A written default clause. If payments stop, use of the name ends and payments made are not refunded. State the grace period in days.
  • No transfer mid-plan. Portfolio sellers who offer plans say the same in their own terms: the name cannot move to another registrar until every payment has cleared.

Escrow.com’s domain holding service is one way to arrange this at arm’s length. Its published terms run from a minimum of three months to a maximum of five years, and it charges for changes to the schedule or to the name’s technical settings during the term, so agree the schedule properly at the start.

What the buyer should understand

Put these in plain words on the listing, because a buyer who misunderstands will resent you later:

  • They are building on a name they do not yet own. If they stop paying, the site and email on it stop too.
  • They can usually pay the balance early.
  • They can usually walk away; they simply lose what they have paid.

When not to offer a plan

Skip it on names under a few hundred dollars, where the administration costs more than the benefit. Be careful with buyers you cannot identify. And do not agree a term so long that renewals, platform fees and your own attention eat the premium.

Do it now. Work out the monthly figure.
