Working with a domain broker

5 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

A broker is worth the fee when the hard part of the sale is finding one particular buyer and getting them to a number. For most names that is not the hard part, and a marketplace listing does the job for less.

What a broker actually does

Bill Sweetman’s guide for GoDaddy describes the work plainly. A broker sets a realistic price expectation, tracks down likely buyers or a hidden owner, runs the negotiation, and oversees the payment and transfer. On the selling side, the part that matters most is proactive: building a list of the most probable buyers and contacting them, instead of waiting for someone to stumble on the name.

Brokers work for one side. A seller’s broker is paid to get you the highest price. A buyer’s broker is paid to acquire a name, often anonymously, for the least. Know which you are talking to.

What it costs

DomainInvesting.com’s directory of brokers puts typical commission at 10% to 20% of the sale price. Published examples sit inside that range: Saw.com lists full-service brokerage at 15%, and Dynadot charges 15% for outbound brokerage, which it reserves for names it judges to be worth roughly $200,000 or more.

That threshold is the useful signal. Outbound work is expensive in hours. A broker takes it on when the commission can repay those hours, which generally means five figures at the least.

When a broker makes sense

  • The name is a one-word .com, a short acronym or a category-defining phrase.
  • The plausible buyers are large companies you cannot reach yourself.
  • You have had serious inquiries and suspect you are leaving money on the table.
  • You do not want your identity known in the negotiation.

It does not make sense for a name priced in the low thousands, for a name with no identifiable buyer, or as a way to avoid setting a price.

What the agreement should say

  • Exclusivity and its length. Nearly every broker requires it. Ninety days is common; a year is long. Ask what happens to buyers the broker contacted if they come back after the term.
  • Commission and what it is charged on. The full sale price, or the price net of escrow fees.
  • Your reserve. The price below which the broker will not bring you offers, and whether it can be revised.
  • Escrow. The guide above recommends a licensed escrow service even where a brokerage offers its own.
  • Confidentiality. Whether the price may be reported publicly after the sale. Reported sales help the broker; they may or may not help you.

Choosing one

Ask for sales of similar names at similar prices, not their biggest deal. Ask who will do the work, since at larger firms it may not be the person you spoke to first. The Internet Commerce Association publishes best practices for brokers, and a broker who has never heard of them is telling you something.

Briefing them well

Give the broker every past inquiry, with dates and amounts. Tell them your real floor. Share anything that affects value: traffic, old trademarks, previous use. Then answer their messages quickly. Deals at this level die of delay more often than of price.

Do it now. Check whether your name needs a broker.
