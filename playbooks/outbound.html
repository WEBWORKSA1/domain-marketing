Outbound that gets replies instead of blocks

7 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

Outbound has a bad name because most of it is bad: hundreds of identical emails to scraped addresses. Done properly it is the opposite. It is ten or twenty messages to companies for whom the name is plainly useful, written as one business person to another.

Finding the right ten

Good prospects share one trait: you can finish the sentence “this name helps them because…” with something specific.

  • The upgrade. A company operating on a longer or clumsier version of the same name: a prefix like “get” or “try”, a hyphen, a lesser extension.
  • The exact match. Businesses whose main product is the phrase in the name.
  • The advertiser. Companies already paying for search ads on the keyword. They have told you what the phrase is worth to them.
  • The local operator. For city-plus-service names, the established firms in that city.

Then find a person. Founder, head of marketing or brand lead. An email to “info@” is a coin toss; an email to the person whose job the name affects is a conversation.

The email

Keep the first message under about 90 words and give it four parts: what you own, why it fits them in one sentence, how a purchase would work, and an easy way to say no.

  • Use a plain subject: the domain name itself works.
  • State the price, or say you are open to an offer. Hiding both wastes a round trip.
  • Mention escrow. It answers the question “how do I know this is real?” before it is asked.
  • No urgency you invented. No “other buyers are circling” unless they are.

Follow up twice, then stop

Most replies come from the follow-up, because the first email arrived on a bad day. Send a short second note after about four working days and a closing note a week later. Three touches is persistent. Five is a nuisance, and it trains spam filters to treat your address as one.

The rules you have to follow

This is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ by country.

  • United States. The CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, including one-to-one business email. Use accurate sender details and subject lines, include a valid postal address, and honour any request to stop.
  • Canada. The anti-spam law there is consent-based. Implied consent can cover a business address that was conspicuously published without a no-solicitation notice, when your message relates to the recipient’s business role. You must still identify yourself and include a working unsubscribe mechanism. The conditions are narrow, so read the regulator’s guidance before you write to Canadian companies.
  • Europe and the United Kingdom. Data protection and e-privacy rules apply to named individuals’ addresses, and member states treat business email differently. When in doubt, use the company’s published contact form.

The line you must not cross

Never offer a name to the owner of a matching trademark. Under the dispute policy that governs most extensions, registering or holding a name mainly to sell it to the trademark owner is listed as evidence of bad faith. An outbound email to that owner hands them the evidence. If a name only makes sense because of someone else’s brand, the right move is to let it expire, not to pitch it.

Generic words and phrases are different. Offering a dictionary-word name to companies in the industry it describes is ordinary commerce. The test is whether the value comes from the language or from a specific company’s reputation.

Keep score

A simple sheet: company, person, date sent, follow-ups, reply, outcome. After fifty emails you will know your reply rate and which angle earns it. That is worth more than any template.

Do it now. Draft the three emails.
