How to price a domain: three numbers, not one

7 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

Ask five tools what a domain is worth and you get five answers. They are not all wrong. They are answering different questions, and the first job in pricing is to decide which question you are asking.

The three prices

Instant Domain Search’s appraisal tool lays this out more clearly than most: it reports an auction price, a marketplace price and a brokerage price for the same name. The idea is worth borrowing whatever tool you use.

  • The investor price is what another domain investor would pay this week. It is the floor, and it is usually a small fraction of the retail figure, because the buyer needs room to resell.
  • The marketplace price is the buy-now number you would list at and reasonably expect to get from a business that finds the name on its own, some time in the next few years.
  • The end-user price is the opening ask to a specific company that has a clear reason to want this exact name. It is the ceiling, and it only exists when such a company does.

Most pricing arguments are two people quoting different rows of that list at each other.

Finding the marketplace price with comparable sales

The method is the one estate agents use. MediaOptions, a brokerage, describes it well: gather verified past sales, keep only those that match on length, keyword type, extension and industry, adjust for how long ago they happened, and narrow to a range.

  1. Collect. Search a public sales database for your keyword and extension. Aim for ten to twelve sales, with at least four or five that bracket your likely price from above and below.
  2. Filter hard. A two-word .com is not a comparable for a one-word .com. A .net is not a comparable for a .com. Drop anything that differs on more than one dimension.
  3. Weight recent sales. Sort by date. Demand for a keyword moves with technology and fashion, so a 2019 sale tells you less than one from this year.
  4. Read the venue. A sale at an expired-name auction is an investor price. A sale through a broker is closer to an end-user price. Do not average the two.

The method has a known blind spot: many of the largest sales are private and never reach a database. For ordinary names that matters little. For one-word .coms it means the public record understates the market.

Where automated appraisals fit

Automated tools are fast and consistent, which makes them good for sorting a long list and bad as a final answer. GoDaddy’s tool returns an estimated value, a suggested list price and a probability of sale, and says plainly that it looks only at the name, not at any website on it. Dynadot’s own advice is to run several tools, discard the highest and lowest, and treat the rest as a baseline. The scorecard on this site belongs in the same category: a band to test, not a verdict.

One warning. If a “buyer” asks you to pay for an appraisal from a particular service before they will make an offer, stop replying. It is a long-running scam and the buyer does not exist.

Buy-now, make-offer, or both

Sedo says names with a buy-now price sell up to three times faster than names that require negotiation. The reason is friction: a priced name can be bought at midnight by someone with a company card, and an unpriced one needs an email, a reply and a week.

  • Use a fixed price for the bulk of a portfolio: names in the hundreds to low thousands, where speed and volume matter more than the last 20%.
  • Use make-offer with a private floor for names where the right buyer’s budget could be many multiples of any number you would dare to print.
  • Use both, a buy-now price with offers allowed, when you want the anchor and the conversation.

Using comps when the offer is low

Comparable sales also do a job after the inquiry arrives. A buyer who has never bought a domain often opens at a few hundred dollars because that is what registration costs suggest. Sending three or four recent, truly comparable sales reframes the conversation without an argument. Investor Elliot Silver makes the necessary caveat: only cite sales that are really comparable. Quoting a seven-figure outlier against a mid-range name makes you look unserious and can end the conversation.
