Closing the sale safely: escrow, transfer and scams

5 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

Agreeing a price is the middle of a sale, not the end. Money has to move one way and a domain the other, between two parties who have usually never met. Escrow exists to make that safe for both.

How escrow works

Escrow.com, the service most domain sales use, describes the sequence on its domains page. In short:

  1. Buyer and seller agree the terms inside the escrow service.
  2. The buyer pays the escrow company, which holds the money in a trust account.
  3. The seller transfers the domain to the buyer.
  4. The escrow company checks the public registration record to confirm the buyer now holds the name.
  5. Only then is the seller paid.

The seller never sends a domain to someone who has not paid, and the buyer never pays for a domain that does not arrive.

What it costs and who pays

The published fee starts at 2.6% with a $50 minimum for domains up to $5,000 and falls in steps as the price rises. A concierge option, where the escrow company holds the name in its own registrar account during the handover, starts at 5.2%. Who pays is negotiable: the buyer, the seller or a split. Settle it before you agree the price, because on a small sale the fee is a real share of the money.

Marketplaces generally bundle payment handling into their commission, so you do not pay escrow on top.

Moving the name

  • Account push. If buyer and seller use the same registrar, the name moves between accounts in minutes. This is the fastest and least error-prone method, and it is worth asking the buyer to open a free account at your registrar to use it.
  • Registrar transfer. Unlock the name, give the buyer the authorisation code, and they start a transfer at their registrar. Allow five to seven days.

Two things delay transfers: a name registered or transferred very recently may be locked for a period, and a transfer usually adds a year of registration that somebody has to pay for. Check both before promising a date.

Scams aimed at sellers

  • The paid appraisal. A buyer offers a generous price but insists on a valuation from a named service first, at your cost. There is no buyer. The appraisal fee is the scam.
  • The fake escrow site. The buyer proposes an escrow company you have not heard of, with a convincing website. Type the address of a licensed service yourself; never follow a link from the other party.
  • The overpayment. A payment arrives for more than the price with a request to refund the difference. The original payment later reverses.
  • The reversible payment. Card and wallet payments can be disputed for months. For anything above pocket money, use escrow or a marketplace checkout.
  • The verification code. Someone asks you to read back a code sent to your phone or email “to confirm ownership”. It is the reset code for your registrar account.

A closing checklist

  1. Price, currency, who pays the fee and the transfer method agreed in writing.
  2. Transaction opened at the escrow service by you, or checked by you at an address you typed.
  3. Payment confirmed as received by the escrow service, in its dashboard, before you move anything.
  4. Name transferred; buyer confirms; funds released.
  5. Remove the listing everywhere else the same day.

Do it now. See escrow fees against commissions.
