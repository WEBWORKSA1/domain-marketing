What happens when a domain expires

5 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

An expired domain does not become available the next morning. It passes through a sequence that can last more than two months, and for any name worth having it is usually sold at auction before the sequence ends.

The stages

  1. Expiry. The registration lapses. The website and email normally stop within days. The owner can still renew at the usual price.
  2. Registrar grace period. The registrar holds the name for a period it sets itself, anywhere from zero to 45 days. This is the important window, because most large registrars now send unrenewed names to an auction partner during it. As DomainSherpa’s long-running guide explains, each registrar is tied to one auction service, so where a name is registered decides where it will be sold.
  3. Redemption. If nobody bought the name, it enters a 30-day period in which only the previous owner can recover it, by paying a redemption fee on top of the renewal.
  4. Pending delete. Five days in which nothing can be done.
  5. Release. The registry frees the name. Drop-catching services fire registration requests the instant it happens.

That is the standard pattern for generic extensions such as .com and .net. Country-code extensions set their own rules, and some skip stages entirely.

Expired-name auctions

If a name you want is registered at a registrar with an auction partner, the auction is your route. The name never reaches release if someone bids. Formats vary: Dynadot, for example, describes seven-day auctions that extend by five minutes when a late bid arrives. Create the account, add the name to a watch list and decide your maximum before the last hour, not during it.

Backorders and drop-catching

For names that go all the way to release, you need a backorder: an instruction to a drop-catching service to try to register the name the moment it frees up. You normally pay only if the service succeeds.

Two practical points, both from Domain Name Wire’s comparisons of the main platforms. First, services differ in who may bid once a name is caught: on some, only people who placed a backorder take part in the auction; on at least one large service the auction is open to every customer, so your research can be rewarded with a bidding war. Second, no service wins every name, so serious buyers place the same backorder at more than one.

Is the name worth chasing?

  • Why did it expire? A business that closed is neutral. A name abandoned after a dispute or a penalty is not.
  • What was on it? Look at archived copies of the old site. Spam, counterfeit goods or adult content leave a mark that can take years to fade.
  • Is it somebody’s brand? An expired name that matches a live trademark is a liability, not an asset.
  • Would you register it fresh at ten times the fee? If not, the excitement is about the auction, not the name.

If it is your name that is expiring

Turn on auto-renew for anything you would be sorry to lose and keep the payment card current. If you mean to let a name go, decide on purpose: list it at wholesale a month before expiry and see whether another investor wants it.
