What makes a domain name sell

5 minute read. Reviewed November 26, 2026.

Buyers do not pay for domains. They pay for what a name does: it gets remembered, typed correctly, trusted on sight. Every trait that raises a price is a version of one of those three.

The traits that raise a price

  • Short. Fewer characters mean fewer mistakes and more room on a sign. Value falls off quickly past about ten characters.
  • Real words. One dictionary word is the top of the market. Two words that belong together come next. Three need a reason.
  • Passes the phone test. Say it once to someone and ask them to type it. If they get it wrong, so will customers.
  • The right extension. .com remains the default that needs no explanation. A few others carry weight in particular fields.
  • Commercial meaning. A word tied to a category where companies spend heavily to win customers is worth more than an equally nice word that is not.

The public appraisal tools weigh the same things. GoDaddy lists memorability, word count and how common the phrase is. Instant Domain Search scores length, word type, familiarity, brandability and extension, and gives brandability the heaviest weight.

The traits that lower it

  • Hyphens. People leave them out when typing, which sends visitors to whoever owns the unhyphenated version.
  • Digits mixed with letters. “4” or “for”? Every ambiguity is a lost visit.
  • Filler prefixes. “Get”, “my”, “the” and “try” mark a name as the second choice. The scoring tools penalise them for that reason.
  • Awkward letter joins. Where one word ends with the letter the next begins with, readers stumble.
  • Someone else’s brand. Not a discount but a disqualification. A name that trades on a trademark is a legal problem, not a product.

What the 2026 record shows

DNJournal’s year-to-date chart of the largest publicly reported sales, through mid-November 2026, is led by Shop.com at $99 million and AI.com at $70 million, with Club.com, Green.com and Tokens.com rounding out the top five. Every one is a single short dictionary word or a two-letter term on .com.

Lower down the same chart, .ai names appear repeatedly at six and seven figures. That is the “right extension” rule at work: an extension that happens to spell the name of a fast-growing industry is carrying real weight in that industry and almost none outside it.

The market underneath is still growing. The latest Domain Name Industry Brief counted 411.6 million registrations across all extensions at the end of the third quarter of 2026, up 8.7% on a year earlier. More registered names means fewer good ones left to hand-register, which is the basic reason an aftermarket exists.

Coined names

Invented names follow different rules, because there is no dictionary meaning to lean on. They sell on sound and look: five to eight letters, alternating consonants and vowels, one obvious pronunciation, no accidental meaning in another major language. Curated marketplaces for these names present each with a logo for a reason. A coined name is easier to value once you can see it as a brand.

A rule for buying

Before registering or bidding, name three kinds of business that could use the name as their main address, and say the name aloud to someone who has not seen it written. If you cannot do the first, or they cannot spell the second, keep your money.
