Draft the three emails that sell a domain

Fill in what you know. You get a first email, a follow-up and a closing note, written the way one business person writes to another, plus subject lines to test.

Why the emails look like this

The first email does four things in under about ninety words: says what you own, gives one specific reason it fits the reader, explains how a purchase would work, and offers an easy way to decline. Nothing in it pretends to urgency, and it mentions escrow early because the reader’s first private question is whether you are real.

The follow-up exists because first emails land on bad days. The third note closes the loop and stops. Three touches is the limit the drafts are built around.

Before you send anything

  • Check the company does not hold a trademark that matches the name. If it does, do not write to it.
  • Find a named person: a founder, or whoever leads marketing or brand.
  • Add your postal address and a clear way to opt out if the law where your reader lives requires it. In the United States it does.

The outbound playbook covers who to write to and the rules in more detail.

Questions people ask

Is sending these emails legal?

It depends on where the recipient is and what you include. The United States regulates commercial email, including one-to-one business email, and Canada and European countries have stricter consent rules. The playbook summarises the main points; it is general information, not legal advice.

How many companies should I write to?

Ten to twenty-five that you chose by hand. If you cannot say in one sentence why the name helps a company, leave it off the list.

Should I state the price?

Usually yes for names in the low thousands, because it saves a round of email and filters out people with no budget. For names where the right buyer could pay far more than you would dare print, invite an offer and keep a private floor.
