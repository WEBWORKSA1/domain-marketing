Map what happens after a domain expires

Enter the expiry date from the public registration record. The timeline shows each stage, the date it ends, and where the name stands today.

Each registrar sets its own, from 0 to 45. If you do not know it, 30 is a reasonable planning figure.

The stages in brief

After expiry the registrar holds the name for a grace period it sets itself. If the name is not renewed or sold at the registrar’s auction in that time, it enters 30 days of redemption, when only the previous owner can recover it for an extra fee, then 5 days of pending delete, and then it is released.

For any name with obvious value, the important stage is the first one. Most large registrars send unrenewed names to an auction partner during the grace period, and a name that attracts a bid there never reaches release. Find out where the name is registered, then check that registrar’s auction venue.

Limits

The dates are a planning guide for generic extensions such as .com and .net. Registrars differ, and country-code extensions follow their own rules. The expired domains playbook explains auctions and backorders.

Questions people ask

Where do I find the expiry date?

In the public registration record for the name, which any lookup service or registrar search will show. Look for the registry expiry date.

Can I register the name on the release date myself?

For a name anyone else wants, almost never. Drop-catching services send registration requests in the instant of release. A backorder with one or more of them is the practical route.

The owner’s site is still up after the expiry date. Why?

Some registrars keep services running for part of the grace period, and some owners renew late. An expiry date in the past does not mean the name is available.
